A working instrument for noticing, naming, and staying

You are not the weather.
You are the station that reads it.

Feelings arrive like weather — fast, physical, uninvited, and passing. The skill is not controlling them. It is reading them accurately enough that you get to choose what happens next. This page is built to be used, not read: a dial for finding the precise word, prompts for when the word won't come, a ledger for watching your own patterns, and practices for the part nobody can hand you — being here while it happens.

This is psychoeducation and self-monitoring, not therapy, diagnosis, or treatment. It draws on published research, cited at the bottom with an honest note about how strong each piece of evidence actually is. If something here opens more than you can hold, that is information worth taking to a human clinician.

01 — The instrument

The dial

Six families in the hub ring, ordered the way affect actually varies — pleasant to unpleasant, high energy to low. Tap inward to outward: family, then feeling, then the precise word. Every ring you move outward is a gain in granularity, and granularity is the part that predicts how well people regulate.

02 — When the word won't come

Five rungs to a name

Most people reach for the label first and land on fine, stressed, or bad. Working from the body upward gets you somewhere truer. Take these in order; each rung narrows the next.

1

Find it in your body before you name it

Close your eyes for ten seconds. Where is this? Not what it means — where. Temperature, weight, movement, edges. Does it have a size?

Tap whatever fits — this fills your ledger entry

Interoception — the sense of your own internal state — is where an emotion is actually detected. People who read body signals more accurately identify their emotions more precisely. Starting at the label skips the data.

2

Set two dials: energy and tone

Before the exact word, get the neighbourhood. Is your body wound up or run down? Does this feel pleasant or unpleasant?

Nearly all emotion sorts along these two axes — Russell's circumplex. Two questions with four answers gets you from seventy-two words down to about a dozen, and it is the one step that works even when you are too flooded to think.

3

Take the family the dials point to

Go to the dial and open the highlighted families. Which one makes your shoulders drop half an inch when you read it? That drop is the signal.

You are looking for recognition, not deduction. Gendlin called the thing you are checking against a felt sense: the body confirms or declines a word faster than reasoning does, and the confirmation feels like release.

4

Go one word more precise than feels necessary

Not sad but homesick. Not anxious but dreading. If two words both fit, pick the one that makes you exhale. If none fit, take the closest and add “…and something else I can't name yet.”

Putting a feeling into words measurably dampens the amygdala response and recruits prefrontal regions — the mechanism behind “name it to tame it.” The effect is real but modest, and it works better when you are naming to be accurate than when you are naming to feel better.

5

Ask what it is asking for

Every feeling is a request. What does this one want — contact, rest, information, a boundary, a decision, or just to be witnessed? Then pick one move: change the situation, change where your attention sits, change how you're reading it, or change what your body is doing.

Those four are Gross's regulation families. The order matters: acting earlier in the chain (choosing or shaping the situation) generally costs less than wrestling the feeling after it has fully arrived — and reappraising works, while pushing the feeling down does not.

Check for imposters

Some words feel like feelings but are actually verdicts about what someone else did. They point outward, invite argument, and hide the thing underneath. Trade them down for the feeling plus the need — the move at the heart of Rosenberg's nonviolent communication.

04 — The practice

How to be present

Presence has an operational definition, which is what makes it trainable: paying attention on purpose, in the present moment, without judging what you find — Kabat-Zinn's formulation, and the basis of every mindfulness protocol that has been tested. Three parts, and the third is the one people skip. Judging what you find turns noticing into a performance review.

The one reframe that makes it work

Presence is not an unbroken beam of attention. It is the act of returning. Your mind will leave — that isn't failure, that's the equipment. Each time you notice you've gone and come back, that is one repetition of exactly the muscle you're building. A ten-minute sit with forty returns is a better session than one with four, because you noticed thirty-six more times. Count returns, not stillness.

5 minutes

Cyclic sighing

Two inhales through the nose — a long one, then a short top-up — then a slow, complete exhale through the mouth. Emphasis on the exhale. Follow the ring.

5:00ready

Balban et al., Cell Reports Medicine, 2023 — five minutes daily for a month improved mood and lowered resting respiratory rate more than mindfulness meditation in the same trial. The strongest short-practice evidence on this page.

3 minutes

The breathing space

Three sixty-second stages, in this order. Wide: what is here right now — thought, feeling, sensation? Name it. Narrow: all attention to the breath at one point. Wide again: the whole body, breath included, held together.

3:00ready

The three-minute breathing space, from mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (Segal, Williams & Teasdale). MBCT roughly halves relapse risk in recurrent depression — one of the better-evidenced psychological interventions there is.

10 minutes

Anchor and return

Pick one anchor — breath at the nostrils, feet on the floor, sound in the room. Rest there. When you notice you've wandered, press the button and come back. The press is the practice.

10:00ready

0 returns

Focused-attention meditation. Meta-analyses find moderate benefit for anxiety, depression and pain, and weaker or unclear evidence for most other claims (Goyal et al., JAMA Internal Medicine, 2014). Useful, not magic.

And when the feeling is too big to sit with

Presence practice is for weather you can stand in. When you're flooded, the order changes: settle the body first, name second, decide third. Never negotiate, reply, or strategize at peak activation — the part of you that does that isn't online. Slow, extended exhales for two minutes; feet on the floor; something cold in your hands. Then come back to the dial. And be kind about the flooding itself: self-compassion — treating your own difficulty the way you'd treat a friend's — is one of the more consistent predictors of recovering well, and self-criticism reliably makes it worse (Neff; MacBeth & Gumley, 2012).

05 — Passing it on

Teaching the gift

Here is the hard part, said plainly: presence is caught, not taught. People — children especially — read your nervous system before they hear your words, and they calm down by borrowing regulation from someone who has it. Which means the first move in teaching this is never a lesson. It's arriving settled.

Move one

Regulate yourself first

Before you say anything, take one slow exhale and drop your shoulders. Co-regulation is physiological: a settled adult is the intervention. An adult performing calm while braced underneath gets read as danger, because the body is the honest channel.

Move two

Name, don't fix

Offer a guess with a question mark on the end — “that sounds lonely, is that close?” — and let them correct you. Being corrected is the win. A guess invites; a diagnosis closes. Naming *for* someone teaches them to outsource the reading you're trying to build.

Move three

Leave the silence

Count three full seconds after they stop. The real sentence usually lives in second four. High-quality listening — undivided attention, no rush to advise — measurably lowers a speaker's defensiveness and helps them think in more complexity about their own situation.

Move four

Ask “what else?” twice

Most people give the acceptable feeling first and the true one third. Two rounds of “what else is in there?” costs you fifteen seconds and gets you past the presentable answer.

With a young child · six years old, crying, won't say why

YouCome sit. I'm not going anywhere. (Sit down. Match their height. Say nothing for five seconds.)
Child(still crying)
YouSomething feels really big right now. Big feelings are allowed here.
YouWhere do you feel it? Point to it. Is it in your tummy or your chest or your throat?
ChildHere. (chest)
YouChest. Is it heavy like a rock, or fast like a bird?
ChildHeavy.
YouHeavy in the chest. That's usually sad. Does sad sound right, or is it a different one?

Body before words, because a six-year-old can point long before they can label. Two concrete choices instead of an open question. And the offered word ends in a real question — they get to say no. Every round of this builds the vocabulary they'll use at sixteen.

With a teenager · one-word answers, door mostly closed

YouYou don't have to talk. I'm going to sit here a minute. (Sit. Do not look directly at them. Say nothing.)
TeenI'm fine.
YouOkay. (Three seconds.) Fine's a fine answer. It's also usually a lid.
TeenIt's just school.
YouIs it more the wound-up kind of bad, or the flattened kind?
Teen…Flattened.
YouFlattened. Is there something else under it?
TeenI don't know. Kind of like nobody would notice if I didn't show up.
YouThat sounds lonely. I'm not going to try to talk you out of it. Thank you for telling me the real one.

The wound-up-or-flattened question is rung two of the ladder — arousal before label, which a teenager will answer when “how are you feeling?” gets nothing. Note what you don't do at the end: no reassurance, no counter-evidence, no fixing. Contradicting the feeling is what teaches them not to bring you the next one. Say what you'll do later; right now, just stay.

With an adult · a colleague, mid-spiral, at your door

ThemSorry — do you have a second? This whole thing is falling apart and I don't even know where to start.
YouI've got ten minutes and I'm not doing anything else with them. (Close the laptop. Visibly.)
Them(seven minutes of it)
YouWhat else is in there?
ThemHonestly? I think I'm embarrassed. I should have caught this in June.
YouThere it is. That's a heavier thing to carry than the deadline is. Do you want help thinking, or do you want a witness right now? Either's fine.

Closing the laptop does more work than any sentence — attention has to be visible to count. “What else is in there?” is what got you from overwhelmed to ashamed, which is the feeling actually driving the spiral. And the last question is the most respectful one you can ask an adult: most unwanted advice is given to people who came for company.

Four things that undo it

All four are well-meant. All four teach the person that their feeling was a problem for you.

“At least it's not…”

Comparison is a hand over the mouth. It says the size of your feeling is the thing we're negotiating.

Instead“That's a lot. Tell me more about it.”

“Don't be sad — look at all this.”

Counter-evidence is an argument, and nobody has ever been argued out of a feeling. They just stop reporting it.

Instead“Sad makes sense here. I'm not going anywhere.”

“You're just tired.”

Naming for them, with no question mark. Even if you're right, you've taken the reading they needed to do themselves.

Instead“Could some of this be tiredness? Or is that not it?”

“Everyone share a feeling. Marcus, you're up.”

Required disclosure isn't emotional learning, it's compliance — and it teaches the quiet ones to produce an acceptable feeling on demand.

InsteadOffer the wheel, model one yourself, allow “pass.”

For a classroom, a group, or a family table

Ninety seconds, at the same moment every day. The consistency is doing most of the work; the content matters less than the fact that it happens whether or not anything is wrong. Brackett's framing is the one to keep in your head — the goal is to raise emotion scientists, curious about what a feeling is and what it's pointing at, rather than emotion judges, sorting feelings into allowed and not allowed.

0:00  Everyone finds it in the body. Silent. No words yet.

0:20  Two dials, on fingers: energy high or low, tone pleasant or not.

0:40  One word each, around the room. “Pass” is a complete answer. You go first, and you go honest — a leader who only ever reports good has taught the whole room the real rule.

1:10  No commentary on anyone's word. Not even praise — praising a “good share” makes it a performance. Just: “thank you.” Then move on.

One caution worth stating: school-based social-emotional programmes show real average benefits on emotional skills and behaviour, but effects are small to moderate and depend heavily on whether adults actually run them as designed. The ritual works because it is boringly consistent, not because it is inspiring.

06 — Showing the work

What the evidence actually says

Sorted by how much weight each idea can bear. Nothing in the third tier is useless — clinical practice runs ahead of its evidence base constantly — but you should know which tier you're standing on, especially before teaching it to someone else.

Well supported

  • Putting a feeling into words dampens the brain's threat response.

    Lieberman et al. (2007), Psychological Science — affect labeling reduced amygdala activity and increased right ventrolateral prefrontal activity. Reviewed in Torre & Lieberman (2018), Emotion Review, which frames it as implicit regulation and notes the effects are modest.

  • People who describe their feelings more precisely regulate them better.

    Emotional granularity / differentiation: Barrett et al. (2001); Kashdan, Barrett & McKnight (2015), Current Directions in Psychological Science. Low granularity is associated with heavier drinking, more aggression after provocation, and poorer outcomes across several clinical populations.

  • Reappraising a situation works; pushing the feeling down doesn't.

    Gross's process model (1998; 2015, Psychological Inquiry). Expressive suppression reduces outward expression while leaving or raising physiological arousal and impairing memory; reappraisal reduces the experience itself.

  • Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy substantially cuts depressive relapse.

    Kuyken et al. (2016), JAMA Psychiatry — individual-patient-data meta-analysis. Broader mindfulness claims are weaker: Goyal et al. (2014), JAMA Internal Medicine, found moderate evidence for anxiety, depression and pain and low or insufficient evidence for most else.

  • Brief structured breathwork with a long exhale improves mood.

    Balban et al. (2023), Cell Reports Medicine — five minutes daily of cyclic sighing over one month beat mindfulness meditation on positive affect and respiratory rate. Randomised, but a single trial with a modest sample.

  • Self-compassion predicts better mental health; self-criticism predicts worse.

    MacBeth & Gumley (2012), Clinical Psychology Review — large inverse association with depression, anxiety and stress across 20 samples. Neff & Germer's Mindful Self-Compassion programme has randomised support.

Promising, with real caveats

  • Emotion sorts along two axes: pleasantness and activation.

    Russell (1980), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. Robust as a description of how people report feelings; it is a map of experience, not proof of two underlying mechanisms.

  • Reading your own body's signals supports naming feelings accurately.

    Interoception research — Critchley & Garfinkel; Mehling's MAIA scale. Directionally well supported, but measurement is contested and heartbeat-detection tasks correlate only loosely with everyday emotional skill.

  • Being listened to well makes people less defensive and more nuanced.

    Itzchakov & Kluger's experimental work on high-quality listening; Rogers (1957) on empathic understanding and unconditional positive regard as therapeutic conditions. Mostly short-term laboratory and field effects.

  • Writing about difficult experience has measurable benefits.

    Pennebaker's expressive writing paradigm — typically 15–20 minutes over three or four days. Meta-analytic effects are small and inconsistent across outcomes; helpful for some people, not a general prescription.

  • Distancing from yourself — third person, or “you” — reduces reactivity.

    Kross & Ayduk on self-distanced reflection and distanced self-talk. Replicated across several paradigms, with smaller effects in field settings.

  • Teaching emotional skills in schools improves skills and behaviour.

    Durlak et al. (2011) and Taylor et al. (2017) meta-analyses of social-emotional learning; Brackett's RULER approach and Permission to Feel (2019). Small-to-moderate effects, highly dependent on implementation fidelity.

Practice wisdom — thin or contested evidence

  • The feeling wheel itself.

    Willcox (1982), Transactional Analysis Journal — the original, with six families. A clinically useful vocabulary scaffold, not a validated instrument or a map of discrete brain systems. Which family a word belongs to is a convention, and constructionist accounts (Barrett) argue emotion categories are built rather than found.

  • Body-mapping of specific emotions.

    Nummenmaa et al. (2014) found consistent self-reported bodily topographies for emotions, but there is no reliable one-to-one signature. Treat the body notes here as prompts for your own noticing, not as a lookup table.

  • Grounding scripts and four-step frameworks (5-4-3-2-1, RAIN, felt-sense focusing).

    Widely used, widely helpful, barely tested in isolation. Gendlin's Focusing has a small supportive literature; RAIN and sensory grounding are clinician-developed heuristics. Use them; don't cite them as evidence.

  • Polyvagal theory as an explanation of why any of this works.

    Deliberately left out of this page. The vocabulary is everywhere in wellness writing, and its core physiological claims have been substantively challenged (Grossman, 2023, Biological Psychology). The practices stand on their own trial evidence without it.

Built as an educational instrument. It does not diagnose, treat, or replace care from a licensed professional, and it does not know anything about you — your ledger stays in your own browser. Persistent low mood, anxiety that shrinks your life, or feelings you cannot get underneath are all reasonable things to bring to a therapist. Wanting to understand yourself better is not a symptom.
